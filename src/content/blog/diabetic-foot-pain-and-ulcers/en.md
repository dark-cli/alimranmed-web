  - type: prose
    heading: Overview
    body: >
      Foot ulcers are a common complication of poorly controlled diabetes. They form as the skin tissue breaks down and
      exposes the layers underneath. They are most common under the big toe and the ball of the foot, and if left
      untreated they can extend all the way to the bone.


      Any person with diabetes can develop foot ulcers and foot pain, but good foot care can help prevent them.
      Treatment depends on the cause, so discuss any foot pain or discomfort with your doctor — an infected sore can
      lead to amputation if it is neglected.
  - type: prose
    heading: Symptoms and diagnosis
    body: >
      One of the first signs of a foot ulcer is discharge from the foot that may stain the socks or seep into the shoe.
      Swelling, irritation, redness, and odour on one or both feet are common early signs.


      The most obvious sign of a serious ulcer is black tissue around the wound. This forms because healthy blood flow
      is not reaching the area. Partial or complete gangrene — tissue death from infection — can appear around the
      ulcer. In this case there may also be foul-smelling discharge, pain, and numbness.


      Signs are not always obvious. Sometimes symptoms only appear once the sore has become infected.
  - type: prose
    heading: Causes
    body: >
      Diabetic foot ulcers are most commonly caused by:


      - **High blood sugar (hyperglycaemia)** — sustained high glucose slows wound healing, so managing blood sugar is
      critical.

      - **Nerve damage** — a long-term effect of diabetes that can lead to loss of sensation in the feet. Damaged nerves
      may feel tingling and painful at first; later, reduced sensitivity means small injuries go unnoticed and can turn
      into painless wounds that develop into ulcers. See also [diabetic neuropathy](/treatments/diabetic-neuropathy/).

      - **Irritated or broken skin on the foot** — minor trauma that would normally heal quickly instead persists.

      - **Poor circulation** — a form of vascular disease in which blood does not flow efficiently to the feet, making
      ulcers slower and harder to heal.
  - type: prose
    heading: "Treatment: the therapeutic triangle"
    body: |
      Treatment of diabetic foot ulcers rests on three combined elements:

      1. **Regulating cumulative blood sugar** — bringing HbA1c and daily glucose under control.
      2. **Treating infection** — antibiotics and local wound cleaning.
      3. **Physiotherapy** — an indispensable complement to the medical management, not an optional add-on.
