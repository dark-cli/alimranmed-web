  - type: prose
    heading: How radiofrequency neurotomy works
    body: >
      Radiofrequency neurotomy — also called [radiofrequency ablation](/services/radiofrequency/radiofrequency/) or
      lesioning — is a minimally invasive procedure that can give lasting relief to patients with facet joint pain.
      Multiple clinical studies show that it significantly reduces the severity and frequency of pain for one to two
      years in the majority of patients.


      The procedure applies heat to specific nerve pathways to "shut off" the transmission of pain signals to the brain.
      It is done on an outpatient basis and needs only local anaesthetic and mild sedation, avoiding the complications
      of open surgery and general anaesthesia. It has a high success rate and a low complication rate.
  - type: prose
    heading: Which conditions we treat with RFA
    body: >
      RFA is used for patients with chronic (long-lasting) low-back and neck pain, and for pain from degeneration of
      joints in arthritis. In practice this covers:


      - Chronic low back pain from facet joint arthritis

      - Chronic neck pain from cervical facet joints

      - [Knee](/services/radiofrequency/knee/) osteoarthritis pain, via genicular nerve ablation

      - [Spinal](/services/radiofrequency/spine/) pain that has not responded to conservative care or steroid injections
  - type: panels
    heading: Thermal vs pulsed radiofrequency
    intro: >-
      There are two types of radiofrequency ablation. The choice depends on the nerve being targeted and the goal of
      treatment.
    panels:
      - eyebrow: TYPE A
        title: Thermal radiofrequency
        items:
          - Tissue temperature reaches up to 80°C
          - Creates a heat lesion that interrupts the nerve
          - Used for facet joint denervation and other sensory nerves
          - Longer-lasting pain relief
      - eyebrow: TYPE B
        title: Pulsed radiofrequency
        items:
          - Tissue temperature stays at or below 45°C
          - Modulates the nerve without destroying it
          - Preferred near mixed motor-sensory nerves
          - Lower risk of numbness or deafferentation

  - type: prose
    heading: Is RFA safe? Who should not have it
    body: >
      RFA has proven to be a safe and effective way to treat certain forms of pain. It is generally well tolerated, with
      very few associated complications. There is a slight risk of infection and bleeding at the insertion site; your
      doctor will advise on your particular risk.


      As with any medical procedure, RFA is not appropriate for everyone. It is not recommended for people who have
      active infections or bleeding disorders. Your doctor can confirm whether RFA is right for you.
  - type: prose
    heading: How to prepare for the procedure
    body: >
      A few precautions the day of the procedure:


      - Do not eat within six hours of your appointment; clear liquids are allowed up to two hours before.

      - If you have diabetes and use insulin, adjust your insulin dose the day of the procedure — your primary-care
      doctor will help with the adjustment. Bring your diabetes medication with you to take after the procedure.

      - Continue all other medications with a small sip of water. Bring them with you so you can take them after the
      procedure. Do not stop any medication without first consulting your primary or referring doctor.

      - Arrange someone to drive you home afterwards. Do not drive or operate machinery for at least 24 hours after the
      procedure.
  - type: prose
    heading: What happens during the procedure
    body: >
      You will first meet the doctor for an evaluation. If RFA is recommended, the doctor will explain the procedure in
      detail — including possible complications and side effects — and answer any questions you have.


      An intravenous (IV) line may be placed in your arm, and local anaesthetic with mild sedation is used to keep you
      comfortable. You may stay awake during the procedure to help assess nerve targeting.


      After the local anaesthetic (you will be awake but pain-free), the doctor inserts a small needle into the area of
      your pain. Using X-ray or ultrasound guidance, the needle is directed to the exact target. A microelectrode is
      then passed through the needle to begin the stimulation phase — you will be asked whether you feel a tingling
      sensation, which helps confirm the electrode is in the optimal spot.


      Once needle and electrode placement are verified, a small radiofrequency current is passed through the electrode
      into the surrounding tissue, heating it to the treatment temperature. You should not feel discomfort during the
      heating phase.
  - type: prose
    heading: After the procedure and going home
    body: |
      Immediately after RFA:

      - You stay in a recovery room for observation while a nurse checks your blood pressure and pulse.
      - A dressing is placed over the injection site.
      - The nurse offers you a drink and reviews your discharge instructions.
      - Someone must drive you home.

      For the first day or two:

      - Do not drive or operate machinery for at least 24 hours.
      - You may resume your normal diet straight away.
      - Avoid strenuous activity for the first 24 hours.
      - No bath for one to two days — showering is fine.
      - You can remove any dressings the evening after the procedure.
  - type: prose
    heading: Side effects and warning signs
    body: >
      You may notice the following after RFA:


      - **Leg numbness.** If your leg feels numb, walk only with assistance. This should last only a few hours and is
      due to the local anaesthetic.

      - **Mild back discomfort.** This may appear as the local anaesthetic wears off and usually lasts two or three
      days. Apply ice to the area on the day of the procedure and moist heat the following day if discomfort persists.
      Your usual pain medications can be used.


      **When to call us.** If you feel severe pain at the injection site, notice swelling or redness, or develop
      increased leg weakness, contact your doctor — you need to be evaluated for bleeding or injection-related
      complications.
