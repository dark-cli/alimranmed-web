  - type: prose
    heading: rTMS and Brain Plasticity
    body: >
      Brain-Derived Neurotrophic Factor (BDNF) has an important role in [neuronal
      plasticity](/services/rehabilitation/neuroplasticity/). Research shows that the combination of rehabilitation and
      low-frequency [rTMS](/services/brain-stimulation/tms/) improves motor function in affected limbs by activating
      BDNF.


      Adult neurogenesis plays critical roles in synaptic plasticity and memory formation. Studies show that rTMS
      stimulation increases adult neurogenesis. In preclinical studies of vascular dementia models, rTMS improved
      cognitive deficits by modifying hippocampal synaptic plasticity and increasing BDNF expression.


      Low-frequency rTMS promotes hippocampal synaptic plasticity through increased expression of neuroprotective Bcl-2
      and reduced expression of pro-death Bax proteins. These mechanisms suggest rTMS could restore impaired brain
      plasticity in dementia patients.
  - type: prose
    heading: Clinical Evidence in Alzheimer's Disease
    body: >
      Cortical excitability is increased in both Alzheimer's disease and vascular dementia. In Alzheimer's disease
      specifically, short-latency afferent inhibition is suppressed—a marker of reduced cortical inhibition.


      Bentwich et al. combined rTMS with cognitive training in Alzheimer's disease patients already treated with
      cholinesterase inhibitors for more than two months. Patients received daily rTMS cognitive training sessions (5
      sessions per week) for 6 weeks, followed by maintenance sessions (2 per week) for an additional 3 months.


      Results demonstrated significant improvement in the Alzheimer Disease Assessment Scale-Cognitive (ADAS-Cog) after
      6 weeks of treatment, suggesting rTMS combined with cognitive training can meaningfully improve cognitive function
      in Alzheimer's patients.
