  - type: highlights
    items:
      - label: What it is
        value: Pain in the lower back — mechanical, disc-related, or (rarely) systemic.
      - label: When to be seen
        value: Pain lasting more than three days, or any leg pain, numbness, or weakness.
      - label: How we treat it
        value: >-
          Assessment first, then a stepped plan: activity, physiotherapy, targeted injection, surgery only when
          indicated.
  - type: prose
    heading: Why most back pain doesn't need a scan
    body: >
      Eight in ten people will have back pain at some point in life. In most cases the pain is mechanical — muscle,
      ligament, or disc origin — and resolves within days to a few weeks with movement and simple analgesia.


      Imaging the low back too early is a common mistake. MRI scans find changes in almost every adult over forty;
      treating the scan rather than the patient leads to unnecessary procedures. The first appointment is a clinical
      examination — imaging is ordered only when the history or exam raises a specific concern.
  - type: prose
    heading: When back pain is a red flag
    body: >
      A small subset of back pain has a serious underlying cause and needs urgent assessment. Any of the following
      warrants same-week evaluation: pain following significant trauma, unexplained weight loss, night pain that wakes
      you, fever, saddle-area numbness, difficulty passing urine, or progressive leg weakness.


      Leg pain, numbness, or weakness — with or without back pain — points to nerve root involvement and shifts the
      assessment away from muscle-and-ligament territory towards disc or spinal stenosis.
  - type: panels
    heading: Two common presentations
    intro: Most patients arrive with one of two patterns. The distinction shapes both the examination and the treatment plan.
    panels:
      - eyebrow: Pattern 1
        title: Mechanical low back pain
        items:
          - Pain localised to the lower back, worse with movement, better with rest.
          - No leg symptoms, no red flags.
          - Typically settles within 2–6 weeks with activity + physiotherapy.
      - eyebrow: Pattern 2
        title: Radicular pain (sciatica)
        items:
          - Pain radiates down one leg, sometimes with numbness or weakness.
          - Usually a disc herniation compressing a nerve root.
          - Most improve without surgery over 6–12 weeks; injection can accelerate that.
  - type: stats
    heading: What we see in clinic
    items:
      - value: 80%
        label: Adults affected by back pain at some point in life
      - value: 90%
        label: Resolve within six weeks with conservative care
      - value: 5%
        label: Require surgical evaluation on first review
  - type: facts
    items:
      - Most back pain is mechanical; imaging is not needed at the first visit.
      - MRI is ordered when leg symptoms are present or a red flag is raised, not to reassure.
      - Bed rest beyond one to two days worsens outcomes.
      - Injection is a diagnostic and therapeutic tool — a good response confirms the pain source.
      - >-
        Surgery for back pain alone (without nerve compression) rarely helps; the decision belongs to the patient after
        full information.
  - type: panels
    heading: How it is treated
    intro: >-
      Care is stepped: start conservative, escalate on evidence, reserve surgery for defined indications. Most patients
      never reach the last group.
    note: The plan is written and given to the patient and the referring clinician at the end of the first visit.
    panels:
      - title: Movement first
        subtitle: Days 1 to 14
        items:
          - Stay active; avoid prolonged bed rest
          - Simple analgesia if needed
          - Heat, gentle stretching
          - Return to work as tolerated
      - title: Physiotherapy
        subtitle: Weeks 2 to 8
        items:
          - Assessment of posture, movement, and core strength
          - Targeted exercise programme
          - Manual therapy where appropriate
          - Ergonomic and work advice
      - title: Interventional pain
        subtitle: When conservative care stalls
        items:
          - Epidural steroid injection (interlaminar or transforaminal)
          - Facet joint injection
          - Radiofrequency ablation for confirmed facet pain
          - Sacroiliac joint injection
      - title: Surgery
        subtitle: Defined indications only
        items:
          - Progressive neurological deficit
          - Cauda equina syndrome (emergency)
          - Persistent radicular pain unresponsive to 6+ weeks of care
          - Instability or structural deformity
