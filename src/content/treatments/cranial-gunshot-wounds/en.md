  - type: prose
    heading: "Cranial gunshot wounds"
    body: |
      Gunshot wounds to the head are penetrating traumatic brain injuries with a mortality rate exceeding 90% in civilian settings — most victims die before reaching medical care. Among those who survive to hospital, management requires rapid assessment, aggressive resuscitation, and individualised decision-making about neurosurgical intervention.

      **Pathophysiology**

      A projectile entering the skull causes injury through several mechanisms:
      - *Direct tissue destruction* along the bullet track
      - *Cavitation* — the temporary cavity created by the projectile's energy transfer, which is many times larger than the bullet diameter, causing widespread destruction of surrounding brain tissue
      - *Haemorrhage* — intracerebral haematoma, intraventricular haemorrhage, or subarachnoid haemorrhage
      - *Cerebral oedema* — diffuse swelling in response to injury that raises intracranial pressure over hours to days
      - *Infection* — retained bone fragments and foreign material create a nidus for abscess formation

      **Emergency assessment and decision-making**

      Three factors guide surgical decision-making on presentation:
      1. *Level of consciousness* — assessed with the Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS). Patients with GCS 3–5 and no evidence of a treatable haematoma have extremely poor prognoses; aggressive surgical intervention is rarely beneficial
      2. *Brainstem neurological function* — pupils, ocular movements, and motor responses. Absent brainstem function indicates irreversible injury
      3. *CT findings* — location and extent of haematoma, trajectory of the bullet through the brain, ventricular involvement, and degree of midline shift

      **Bullet trajectory and prognosis**

      Trajectories through the brainstem, multiple cerebral lobes, or the ventricular system are almost uniformly fatal. Tangential or frontal lobe trajectories in patients with preserved consciousness carry a substantially better prognosis. Many initial survivors develop uncontrolled intracranial hypertension in the days following injury.

      **Surgical management**

      - *Craniotomy and debridement:* removal of accessible bone fragments, devitalised brain tissue, and the bullet where feasible; evacuation of haematoma causing significant mass effect
      - *Decompressive craniectomy:* removal of a large bone flap to allow the swollen brain to expand without fatal herniation in selected patients with refractory intracranial hypertension
      - *ICP monitoring:* intraparenchymal or intraventricular pressure monitors guide intensive care management

      **Rehabilitation**

      Survivors of cranial gunshot wounds require prolonged, specialised neurorehabilitation addressing motor deficits, aphasia, cognitive impairment, behavioural changes, and epilepsy (which develops in a significant proportion). Neuromodulatory therapies including rTMS and physiotherapy form part of the post-acute rehabilitation programme at Alimran Medical Center.
