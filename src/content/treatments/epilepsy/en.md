  - type: prose
    heading: "What epilepsy is"
    body: |
      Epilepsy is a disorder of recurrent, unprovoked seizures. A single seizure does not make epilepsy — the defining feature is tendency to recur. In most patients the seizures are generalised (affecting both brain hemispheres) or focal (starting in one region and sometimes spreading).

      The causes vary widely. Many cases are genetic or developmental. Others arise from head trauma, stroke, brain tumour, or infection. In many cases the cause remains unknown even after investigation.

  - type: comparison_pair
    heading: "Generalised vs focal seizures"
    intro: "The seizure type guides investigation and treatment choice. Some patients have both."
    a:
      label: "Type A"
      title: "Generalised seizures"
      items:
        - "Affect both hemispheres from onset (or within seconds)."
        - "Loss of consciousness is typical."
        - "Tonic-clonic (stiffening and shaking), absence (blank stares), or atonic (sudden falls) patterns."
        - "EEG abnormality is bilateral and diffuse."
    b:
      label: "Type B"
      title: "Focal seizures"
      items:
        - "Start in one brain region, may or may not spread."
        - "Awareness can be preserved (focal aware) or lost (focal impaired awareness)."
        - "Hand movements, lip smacking, or automatisms are common."
        - "EEG abnormality is localised."

  - type: stats_facts
    heading: "What we see in clinic"
    stats:
      - value: "1%"
        label: "Of the population has active epilepsy at any given time"
      - value: "70%"
        label: "Of new-onset seizures respond to a single anti-seizure drug"
      - value: "30%"
        label: "Are drug-resistant and may benefit from surgery"
    facts:
      - "The EEG is the main investigation — it shows abnormal discharges during or between seizures."
      - "MRI is essential to look for structural causes (scar, tumour, malformation)."
      - "Video-telemetry — recording the patient during a seizure — is the gold standard for locating the seizure focus before surgery."
      - "Driving restrictions apply in most countries; seizure freedom (usually 6–12 months) must be confirmed before licensing resumes."
      - "Sudden Unexpected Nocturnal Death in Epilepsy (SUDEP) is rare but serious — risk falls with seizure control."

  - type: treatment_groups
    heading: "How it is treated"
    intro: "Anti-seizure medication is first-line. Surgery is the next step for drug-resistant epilepsy."
    note: "Seizure freedom is the goal, but some patients settle for a significant reduction if complete freedom is not achievable."
    groups:
      - title: "Anti-seizure medication"
        subtitle: "First-line for all new-onset seizures"
        items:
          - "Monotherapy (one drug) tried first — leveteracetam, sodium valproate, lamotrigine, or lacosamide depending on seizure type"
          - "If monotherapy fails, a second drug is added (polytherapy)"
          - "Frequent blood tests and EEGs to assess control and side effects"
      - title: "Ketogenic diet"
        subtitle: "Adjunctive for focal or generalised seizures"
        items:
          - "High-fat, low-carbohydrate diet used for decades in paediatric epilepsy"
          - "Works for adults too — about 30% achieve significant seizure reduction"
          - "Requires dietitian supervision and monitoring"
      - title: "Surgery"
        subtitle: "Drug-resistant epilepsy (failure of two or more AEDs)"
        items:
          - "Resection: removal of the seizure-generating zone if focal and surgically accessible"
          - "Disconnection: callosotomy or functional hemispherectomy to prevent spread"
          - "Neuromodulation: vagus nerve stimulation or responsive neurostimulation (RNS) for multifocal or diffuse seizure zones"
      - title: "Counselling"
        subtitle: "Ongoing support throughout treatment"
        items:
          - "Psychoeducation about seizure triggers and lifestyle"
          - "Driving and work implications"
          - "Pregnancy planning (some AEDs carry teratogenic risk)"
