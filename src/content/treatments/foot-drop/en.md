  - type: prose
    heading: What foot drop is
    body: >
      Foot drop — also called drop foot — is the inability or difficulty to raise the front part of the foot (ankle
      dorsiflexion). Rather than a disease in itself, it is a symptom of an underlying problem affecting the
      neurological, muscular, or anatomical structures that control foot lifting.


      The key muscles involved are the ankle dorsiflexors — primarily the tibialis anterior — which are controlled by
      the common peroneal (fibular) nerve, a branch of the sciatic nerve. Damage anywhere along the pathway from the
      brain to the peroneal nerve produces weakness in these muscles and the characteristic foot drop.


      **Causes:**


      *Nerve injury (most common):*

      - Compression of the common peroneal nerve at the fibular neck (the nerve passes superficially here and is
      vulnerable to prolonged leg crossing, squatting, or plaster cast pressure)

      - Nerve root compression in the lumbar spine (L4–L5 disc herniation causing L5 radiculopathy)

      - Damage to the peroneal nerve during hip or knee replacement surgery

      - Peripheral neuropathy (diabetic neuropathy, Charcot-Marie-Tooth disease)


      *Muscle disorders:*

      - Muscular dystrophy

      - Inflammatory myopathy


      *Central nervous system disorders:*

      - Stroke (affecting the motor cortex or corticospinal tract)

      - Multiple sclerosis

      - Amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS)

      - Spinal cord injury or tumour

      - Brain tumour


      **Symptoms and gait characteristics:**

      - Inability to lift the front of the foot when walking — the foot or toes drag along the ground

      - Compensatory high-stepping gait (steppage gait) — the patient lifts the knee excessively to clear the foot from
      the floor, mimicking someone climbing stairs

      - Foot slap — the foot comes down flat or heel-last with an audible slap

      - Instability on uneven surfaces and increased risk of falls and trips

      - Numbness or tingling on the top of the foot and between the first two toes (if the peroneal sensory branch is
      affected)


      **Diagnosis** requires identifying the level of the lesion — peripheral nerve (EMG/nerve conduction studies),
      nerve root (MRI of the lumbar spine), or central (MRI of the brain or spinal cord). EMG can distinguish between a
      peroneal nerve lesion and an L5 radiculopathy.


      **Treatment:**

      - *Treat the underlying cause:* decompress a compressed peroneal nerve; treat the disc herniation; manage the
      stroke or MS

      - *Ankle-foot orthosis (AFO):* a brace holding the foot in a neutral position, enabling safer, more
      energy-efficient walking

      - *Physiotherapy:* stretching of the heel cord (to prevent equinus contracture), strengthening of residual
      dorsiflexors, gait retraining, and balance training

      - *Functional electrical stimulation (FES):* surface electrodes applied over the peroneal nerve stimulate
      dorsiflexion with each step — restores near-normal gait in suitable patients

      - *Neuromodulation:* rTMS and tDCS targeting the motor cortex can augment neurological recovery after central
      lesions

      - *Surgery:* peroneal nerve decompression or neurolysis for compressive injuries; tendon transfer (tibialis
      posterior) for permanent foot drop with no neurological recovery expected
