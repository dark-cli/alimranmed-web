  - type: prose
    heading: What frozen shoulder is
    body: >
      Frozen shoulder — clinically, adhesive capsulitis — is an inflammatory condition in which the capsule around the
      shoulder joint thickens, tightens, and adheres to itself. The result is severe pain and progressive loss of
      movement, particularly external rotation.


      The condition is self-limiting: even without treatment most cases resolve within 18 to 30 months. But that is a
      long time to lose a shoulder, and the point of active treatment is to shorten the runway meaningfully — often to a
      matter of months instead.
  - type: panels
    heading: Three phases you go through
    intro: >-
      The condition moves through three overlapping phases. The right treatment depends on which phase you are in —
      pushing physiotherapy hard in the freezing phase makes pain worse and doesn't restore movement any faster.
    panels:
      - eyebrow: Phase 1
        title: Freezing (pain-dominant)
        items:
          - 2–9 months. Pain is worst; movement gradually declines.
          - "Focus on pain control: injection, gentle mobilisation only."
          - Aggressive stretching now is counter-productive.
      - eyebrow: Phase 2 & 3
        title: Frozen → Thawing
        items:
          - 4–12 months frozen; 5–24 months thawing.
          - Pain reduces; stiffness is the main problem.
          - Focused physiotherapy delivers most of the gain here.
  - type: stats
    heading: What we see in clinic
    items:
      - value: 2–5%
        label: Lifetime prevalence in the general population
      - value: 70%
        label: Improve substantially within 3 months of intra-articular steroid injection
      - value: 10%
        label: Require surgical capsular release for refractory stiffness
  - type: facts
    items:
      - Diabetes, thyroid disease, and prior shoulder immobilisation are the main risk factors.
      - Imaging (X-ray, MRI) is used to exclude other causes; frozen shoulder itself is a clinical diagnosis.
      - The 'freezing' phase is the wrong time for aggressive physiotherapy; pain control first.
      - Intra-articular steroid injection is most effective in the first six months of symptoms.
      - >-
        Manipulation under anaesthesia and arthroscopic capsular release are reserved for cases that remain stuck after
        6+ months of full conservative care.
  - type: panels
    heading: How it is treated
    intro: >-
      Care is phase-matched. Same interventions in the wrong order can slow you down; matched to the phase, they
      measurably shorten the timeline.
    note: The plan is reviewed at 6-week intervals — the phases shift, and the treatment shifts with them.
    panels:
      - title: Pain control
        subtitle: Freezing phase
        items:
          - Simple analgesia + short NSAID course
          - Sleep positioning advice
          - Intra-articular steroid injection (image-guided)
      - title: Physiotherapy
        subtitle: Frozen + thawing phases
        items:
          - Passive range-of-motion techniques
          - Capsular stretching (rotator cuff, pendulum)
          - Home exercise programme, reviewed every 4–6 weeks
      - title: Injection therapy
        subtitle: When pain blocks progress
        items:
          - Suprascapular nerve block
          - Hydro-dilatation (capsular distension with saline + steroid)
          - Repeat intra-articular steroid at 8–12 weeks if needed
      - title: Surgery
        subtitle: Small minority
        items:
          - Manipulation under anaesthesia (MUA)
          - Arthroscopic capsular release
          - Immediate post-op physiotherapy is non-negotiable
