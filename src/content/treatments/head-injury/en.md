    items:
      - label: What it is
        value: >-
          Trauma to the head that may affect the scalp, skull, or brain — ranging from mild concussion to severe
          intracranial haemorrhage.
      - label: Emergency signs
        value: >-
          Loss of consciousness, vomiting, seizure, worsening headache, weakness on one side, unequal pupils, clear
          fluid from nose or ear.
      - label: How we treat it
        value: >-
          Immediate CT to stratify risk; observation, surgical evacuation of significant clots, and structured
          rehabilitation for the deficits that remain.
  - type: prose
    heading: What head injury covers
    body: >
      Head injury is a broad term. At one end sits a mild concussion — no imaging changes, symptoms clear within days.
      At the other sits a major intracranial bleed that needs an emergency operation within the hour. The whole spectrum
      is triaged by the same clinical framework: level of consciousness (Glasgow Coma Scale), pupil examination, focal
      neurology, and — for anything but the mildest injury — a CT scan.


      Getting the CT right is the pivotal decision. Missed bleeds kill; unnecessary CTs cost money and radiation without
      gain. Validated decision rules (Canadian CT Head Rule, NEXUS) tell us which patients need imaging and which can be
      safely observed.
  - type: quote
    text: >-
      Everyone worries about the operation. What matters more is the twelve hours after: seizure, re-bleed, rising
      pressure. The bedside review, not the theatre, is where most patients are saved.
    attribution: Hussein Imran Mousa, consultant neurosurgeon
  - type: panels
    heading: Two intracranial injuries you must not miss
    intro: >-
      The two most time-critical intracranial injuries look different on imaging and behave very differently. Both need
      urgent surgical assessment; the outcome depends on how fast the operation happens.
    panels:
      - eyebrow: Injury A
        title: Extradural haematoma
        items:
          - Bleed between skull and dura, usually from a torn middle meningeal artery.
          - "Classic lucid interval: lose consciousness, wake up, then deteriorate."
          - Excellent outcome if evacuated early; devastating if missed.
      - eyebrow: Injury B
        title: Acute subdural haematoma
        items:
          - Bleed under the dura, typically from torn bridging veins.
          - More common in the elderly and in patients on anticoagulants.
          - Prognosis heavily depends on underlying brain injury, not just the clot.
  - type: stats
    heading: What we see in clinic
    items:
      - value: 80%
        label: Of head injuries are mild — normal CT, symptoms resolve within weeks
      - value: 15%
        label: Are moderate — imaging changes, structured observation and rehab
      - value: 5%
        label: Are severe — surgery, intensive care, long-term rehabilitation
  - type: facts
    items:
      - Any patient on anticoagulants who sustains a head injury needs urgent CT regardless of how minor it seems.
      - >-
        Loss of consciousness, amnesia, seizure, repeated vomiting, or focal neurology all lower the threshold for
        imaging.
      - >-
        Post-concussion syndrome — headache, dizziness, poor concentration — can persist for weeks after a mild injury;
        it does not mean the initial scan was wrong.
      - >-
        Second-impact syndrome is a serious risk in young athletes who return to play before the first concussion has
        fully resolved.
      - >-
        Return-to-driving, return-to-work, and return-to-sport are three separate conversations with different
        timelines.
  - type: panels
    heading: How it is managed
    intro: >-
      Care spans emergency assessment, surgical intervention where indicated, and structured rehabilitation. Most
      patients only need the first two boxes; a minority need the full pathway.
    note: >-
      The acute phase is managed at the emergency department. The clinic sees patients afterwards for follow-up,
      rehabilitation, and secondary prevention.
    panels:
      - title: Emergency assessment
        subtitle: First hours
        items:
          - Glasgow Coma Scale and full neurological examination
          - CT head — always in moderate or severe injury
          - Cervical spine imaging in polytrauma
          - Blood tests including coagulation panel
      - title: Surgery
        subtitle: When indicated
        items:
          - Craniotomy and clot evacuation for extradural or acute subdural haematoma
          - Burr holes for chronic subdural haematoma
          - Decompressive craniectomy for refractory raised intracranial pressure
          - Depressed skull fracture elevation
      - title: Observation & medical care
        subtitle: The majority of cases
        items:
          - Neurological observations every 30 min to 1 hour
          - Repeat CT if any deterioration
          - Anticonvulsant prophylaxis in selected injuries
          - Analgesia and antiemetics
      - title: Rehabilitation
        subtitle: The long tail
        items:
          - Cognitive rehabilitation for memory and attention
          - Physiotherapy for motor recovery
          - Speech and language therapy where affected
          - Vocational assessment for return to work
