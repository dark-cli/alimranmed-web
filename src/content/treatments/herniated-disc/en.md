          The gel-like nucleus of an intervertebral disc protrudes through a tear in its fibrous outer ring, often

      - label: Typical signs
        value: >-
          Sharp pain radiating into an arm or leg (depending on level), with numbness, tingling, or weakness in the same
          distribution.
      - label: How we treat it
        value: >-

  - type: prose
    heading: What a herniated disc is
    body: >
      Intervertebral discs sit between spinal vertebrae, acting as shock absorbers and permitting movement. Each disc
      has a tough fibrous outer ring (annulus fibrosus) and a soft gel-like core (nucleus pulposus). When the outer ring
      tears, the core can push outward, forming a herniation.


      The herniated fragment often compresses a nerve root as it exits the spine, producing the classic radicular pain
      pattern — sciatica in the leg from a lumbar disc, radiating arm pain from a cervical disc. The compression can be
      from mechanical pressure, chemical irritation, or both.
  - type: panels
    heading: Where herniations occur
    intro: >-
      The two locations that dominate clinic — cervical and lumbar — present differently because of what each nerve root
      controls. Thoracic herniations exist but are rare and don't fit the same pattern.
    panels:
      - eyebrow: Level A
        title: Lumbar herniation
        items:
          - Most common at L4/5 and L5/S1.
          - Pain radiates down the buttock and leg (sciatica).
          - Weakness of ankle dorsiflexion (L5) or plantarflexion (S1) if severe.
      - eyebrow: Level B
        title: Cervical herniation
        items:
          - Most common at C5/6 and C6/7.
          - Pain radiates from the neck into the arm and hand.
          - Weakness of biceps (C5/6) or triceps/wrist extension (C6/7) if severe.
  - type: stats
    heading: What we see in clinic
    items:
      - value: 90%
        label: Of lumbar herniations improve without surgery within 6–12 weeks
      - value: 70%
        label: Improve substantially after a single transforaminal epidural injection
      - value: 5%
        label: Require surgical decompression at first review
  - type: facts
    items:
      - Imaging findings and symptoms often don't match — MRI shows disc changes in 30–40% of pain-free adults.
      - Progressive motor weakness or new bladder/bowel dysfunction is a red flag needing same-day surgical assessment.
      - Bed rest beyond one or two days makes recovery slower, not faster.
      - >-
        The natural history is favourable: even severe disc herniations shrink on repeat imaging in most patients over
        months.
      - >-
        Surgery in the first six weeks is reserved for progressive weakness; earlier surgery for pain alone does not
        change long-term outcome.
  - type: panels
    heading: How it is treated
    intro: The plan is stepped and evidence-based. Most patients never leave the first two tiers.
    note: The plan is written and reviewed at 4–6 week intervals; the treatment shifts as symptoms shift.
    panels:
      - title: Movement + medication
        subtitle: Weeks 1 to 4
        items:
          - Stay active; avoid prolonged bed rest
          - Simple analgesia + NSAIDs
          - Short course of neuropathic-pain medication if nerve pain is severe
      - title: Physiotherapy
        subtitle: Weeks 2 to 8
        items:
          - Postural correction and ergonomic advice
          - Nerve gliding, core stability
          - Manual therapy as an adjunct
      - title: Interventional pain
        subtitle: When conservative care stalls
        items:
          - Transforaminal epidural steroid injection
          - Interlaminar epidural injection for multi-level pain
          - Selective nerve root block (diagnostic + therapeutic)
      - title: Surgery
        subtitle: Defined indications only
        items:
          - Microdiscectomy for persistent radicular pain with a matching disc on MRI
          - Endoscopic discectomy for suitable single-level herniations
          - Cervical discectomy and fusion (ACDF) or artificial disc replacement
          - Emergency decompression for cauda equina syndrome
