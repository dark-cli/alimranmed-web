      - label: What it is
        value: >-
          Cramping, aching, or fatigue pain in the calf, thigh, or buttock that begins consistently after walking a
          predictable distance and resolves within minutes of rest. The defining symptom of peripheral artery disease —
          atherosclerotic narrowing of the arteries supplying the legs restricts blood flow below the demand of
          exercising muscle. Pain location reflects the level of arterial blockage: calf (superficial femoral artery),
          thigh (iliac artery), buttock/groin (aortoiliac disease).
      - label: Symptoms
        value: >-
          Pain, cramping, aching, throbbing, or burning in the calf (most common), thigh, or buttocks that reliably
          begins after walking a certain distance and subsides within minutes of standing still. Both legs may be
          affected. Advanced disease causes rest pain, skin discolouration, poor wound healing, and ulceration.
          Aortoiliac disease may also cause erectile dysfunction.
      - label: Approach
        value: >-
          Supervised walking exercise — walking to moderate pain, resting to recovery, repeating for 30–45 minutes, 3+
          times per week — is the most effective non-surgical treatment. Cardiovascular risk reduction (smoking
          cessation, antiplatelet, statin, BP and glucose control) addresses the underlying disease. Spinal cord
          stimulation provides effective pain relief and improves perfusion in refractory limb ischaemia. Angioplasty or
          surgical bypass restores blood flow when conservative measures fail.
  - type: prose
    heading: What intermittent claudication is
    body: >
      Intermittent claudication is the reproducible, exercise-induced limb pain that is the principal symptom of
      peripheral artery disease (PAD). It is described as 'intermittent' because the pain is consistently absent at rest
      and consistently present after a predictable amount of activity. This distinguishes it from other causes of leg
      pain such as musculoskeletal or neurogenic pain, which may be present at rest or vary inconsistently.


      **Mechanism**


      Atherosclerotic plaques narrow the lumen of the peripheral arteries — most often the superficial femoral,
      popliteal, or iliac arteries. At rest, the reduced blood flow is sufficient to meet basal muscle metabolic needs.
      During walking, active muscles require a 5- to 10-fold increase in blood flow; the stenosed arteries cannot
      deliver this increase, leading to oxygen and metabolite accumulation that stimulates pain receptors in the muscle.


      **Pain location and arterial level**


      The location of claudication pain correlates with the arterial segment affected:

      - *Calf pain:* most common; reflects disease in the superficial femoral artery (SFA) or popliteal artery

      - *Thigh pain:* blockage higher in the iliac artery

      - *Buttock and hip pain + erectile dysfunction:* aortoiliac occlusive disease (Leriche syndrome)


      **Severity and natural history**


      Claudication distance — the distance walked before pain begins — is a measure of severity. It tends to improve
      with exercise, remain stable, or slowly worsen over years. A minority of patients progress to critical limb
      ischaemia (rest pain, ulceration, gangrene) requiring urgent revascularisation.


      **Risk factors**


      - Smoking — the strongest and most modifiable risk factor

      - Diabetes mellitus

      - Hypertension and hypercholesterolaemia

      - Age over 50 years, particularly in smokers or diabetics

      - Family history of atherosclerosis or PAD


      **Management**


      *Exercise therapy:* Supervised exercise programmes consistently outperform angioplasty in improving pain-free
      walking distance and quality of life for stable claudication. The mechanism includes improved muscular oxygen
      extraction, collateral development, and endothelial function.


      *Cardiovascular risk reduction:* Antiplatelet therapy (aspirin or clopidogrel), high-dose statin therapy, blood
      pressure control, and smoking cessation reduce the risk of myocardial infarction and stroke — the leading causes
      of death in PAD patients.


      *Spinal cord stimulation:* An established treatment for critical limb ischaemia and refractory claudication not
      amenable to revascularisation — improves microcirculation, reduces rest pain, and promotes wound healing.


      *Revascularisation:* Percutaneous transluminal angioplasty (PTA) with or without stenting, or surgical bypass, is
      indicated for disabling claudication that fails exercise and medical therapy, or for limb-threatening ischaemia.
