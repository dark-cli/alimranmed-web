  - type: prose
    heading: "What muscle cramps are"
    body: |
      A muscle cramp is a sudden, involuntary, sustained contraction of a skeletal muscle or part of a muscle, producing intense localised pain. Most cramps are benign and transient; recurrent or severe cramps — particularly in older adults — warrant investigation for treatable underlying causes.

      **Common sites**

      - Calf (gastrocnemius or soleus) — the most frequent site; the muscle belly goes hard and visible under the skin
      - Thigh (quadriceps or hamstrings)
      - Foot (plantar intrinsic muscles) — arch cramps, often nocturnal
      - Hand and forearm (writer's cramp; occupational cramp in musicians and typists)

      **Causes**

      - *Exercise-related:* overuse of a muscle, fatigue, dehydration, and heat exposure deplete electrolytes and alter motor neurone excitability, triggering cramps during or after activity. Common in athletes and manual workers
      - *Inadequate blood supply (ischaemic cramping):* atherosclerotic narrowing of leg arteries (peripheral artery disease) restricts oxygen delivery to exercising muscles, producing cramp-like pain that consistently appears after a predictable walking distance and resolves with rest — a pattern known as intermittent claudication
      - *Nerve compression (neurogenic cramping):* lumbar spinal stenosis compresses nerve roots supplying the legs, causing cramp-like pain or weakness that worsens with walking upright but improves with forward flexion (e.g., leaning on a shopping trolley)
      - *Electrolyte deficiency:* low levels of potassium, magnesium, or calcium in the blood directly reduce the threshold for muscle fibre depolarisation. Diuretics prescribed for hypertension or heart failure are a common iatrogenic cause
      - *Idiopathic nocturnal leg cramps:* no identifiable cause; common in older adults and pregnant women. Possibly related to shortened calf muscles from prolonged sitting and reduced mobility

      **Risk factors**

      - Older age (reduced muscle mass, poorer circulation)
      - Dehydration and electrolyte depletion, especially in hot weather or intensive exercise
      - Pregnancy (hormonal changes, compression of vascular and neural structures)
      - Diabetes, liver disease, thyroid dysfunction, or kidney failure
      - Medications: diuretics, statins, beta-agonists, raloxifene, nifedipine

      **Self-care**

      - *Immediate relief:* stretch the cramped muscle by extending the knee and dorsiflexing the foot for a calf cramp; hold until the cramp releases
      - *Prevention:* stay well hydrated; maintain adequate dietary intake of potassium (bananas, leafy greens), magnesium (nuts, seeds), and calcium; do calf stretches before sleep for nocturnal cramps
      - *Footwear:* avoid prolonged barefoot walking or flat-soled shoes; raise the foot of the bed slightly

      **Medical management**

      For cramps that fail to respond to self-care or are disabling and recurrent:
      - Treat the underlying cause (vascular, neurological, metabolic)
      - Quinine (limited by side effects) and muscle relaxants (mexiletine, baclofen) are used in refractory cases
      - Botox injection into the affected muscle produces prolonged relaxation and relieves chronic, severely disabling cramps
      - Pulsed radiofrequency and spinal cord stimulation address neurogenic and ischaemic components
