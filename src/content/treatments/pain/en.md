    body: |
      Pain is not a single entity — it is a symptom arising from dozens of distinct mechanisms. Effective management requires identifying which mechanism is dominant in a given patient and targeting it specifically.

      **Types of pain**

      *Nociceptive pain* arises from activation of pain receptors by tissue injury or inflammation. It is typically well-localised and proportionate to the degree of injury — musculoskeletal pain from fractures, arthritis, sprains, and surgical wounds is predominantly nociceptive.

      *Neuropathic pain* arises from injury or disease of the somatosensory nervous system itself. Characteristics include burning or electric quality, allodynia (pain from non-painful stimuli), hyperalgesia, and distributions that follow nerve territory rather than tissue anatomy. Examples include sciatica, diabetic neuropathy, postherpetic neuralgia, and trigeminal neuralgia.

      *Nociplastic pain* (formerly central sensitisation) arises from altered nociception despite no clear evidence of tissue or nerve damage. The nervous system has become sensitised — amplifying signals and generating pain without proportionate peripheral input. Fibromyalgia and many chronic regional pain syndromes involve nociplastic mechanisms.

      **Principles of treatment**

      - Match the treatment to the mechanism: anti-inflammatory strategies for nociceptive pain, neuromodulatory agents and interventions for neuropathic pain, central nervous system-targeted approaches for nociplastic pain
      - Multimodal care consistently outperforms single-modality treatment for chronic pain
      - Physiotherapy and active rehabilitation are essential components — passive treatments alone rarely produce durable improvement
      - Interventional procedures (nerve blocks, Botox, radiofrequency ablation) provide targeted, often prolonged relief for specific conditions
