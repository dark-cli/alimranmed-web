        value: "Pituitary tumours are abnormal growths originating from the anterior or posterior pituitary gland. Over 90% are benign adenomas. Microadenomas are < 1 cm; macroadenomas are ≥ 1 cm and may compress adjacent structures. Functioning tumours secrete hormones in excess — the type of hormone determines the clinical syndrome. Macroadenomas with visual compromise: urgent surgery. Endoscopic transsphenoidal resection is the standard surgical approach. Radiation (stereotactic radiosurgery or IMRT) for residual or recurrent tumours. Hormone replacement for hypopituitarism."
  - type: prose
    heading: "What pituitary tumours are"
    body: |
      The pituitary gland — a pea-sized structure at the base of the skull in the sella turcica — regulates most of the body's endocrine system by secreting tropic hormones that govern the thyroid, adrenal glands, gonads, and growth. Pituitary tumours disrupt this regulation either by oversecreting a specific hormone (functioning tumours) or by compressing and destroying normal pituitary tissue (non-functioning or large tumours causing hypopituitarism).

      **Classification**

      *Microadenomas (< 1 cm):* confined to the sella; rarely cause mass effect. Most present because of hormone overproduction.

      *Macroadenomas (≥ 1 cm):* may extend suprasellarly, compress the optic chiasm (causing visual field defects), invade the cavernous sinuses (cranial nerve palsies), or compress the hypothalamus. Also compress normal pituitary tissue, causing hypopituitarism.

      **Hormone-secreting tumour syndromes**

      *Prolactinoma (most common):* excess prolactin causes galactorrhoea and amenorrhoea in women; hypogonadism, erectile dysfunction, and gynaecomastia in men. Medical treatment with dopamine agonists (cabergoline, bromocriptine) is highly effective and often curative — surgery reserved for drug-resistant or intolerant cases.

      *ACTH-secreting tumour (Cushing's disease):* excess ACTH stimulates cortisol overproduction. Cushing syndrome features: central obesity with moon face and buffalo hump, purple striae, muscle weakness, hypertension, diabetes, osteoporosis, and psychiatric disturbance. Treated surgically; pasireotide, ketoconazole, or mifepristone used as adjuncts.

      *GH-secreting tumour (acromegaly):* excess GH causes progressive enlargement of hands, feet, and facial features; jaw protrusion; increased ring and shoe size. Complications include sleep apnoea, cardiomyopathy, diabetes, and colon polyps. Treated surgically; somatostatin analogues (octreotide, lanreotide) used if surgery fails to normalise GH.

      *TSH-secreting tumour:* the rarest functioning type; causes hyperthyroidism with normal or elevated TSH (tricky diagnosis). Treated surgically.

      **Non-functioning tumours**

      Present with mass effect symptoms — headache and visual field defects. Hypopituitarism from compression of normal pituitary causes fatigue, cold intolerance, amenorrhoea, sexual dysfunction, and diabetes insipidus. Treatment is surgical decompression.

      **Surgical approaches**

      *Endoscopic transsphenoidal resection* (standard approach): the tumour is removed via the nose and sphenoid sinus without external incision; no brain retraction required. Excellent visualisation; appropriate for most tumours.

      *Transcranial (craniotomy):* reserved for very large or invasive tumours with significant suprasellar or lateral extension not accessible transsphenoidally.

      **Radiation therapy**

      Used for residual or recurrent tumours not controlled by surgery or medication:
      - *Stereotactic radiosurgery (Gamma Knife, CyberKnife):* single high-dose, focused; best for small, well-defined residual tumour ≥ 3mm from optic chiasm
      - *Fractionated stereotactic radiotherapy (FSRT) / IMRT:* for larger tumours or those close to the optic apparatus; delivered over 4–6 weeks
      - Benefits may take months to years; risk of hypopituitarism increases with time after radiation
