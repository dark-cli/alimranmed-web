  - type: prose
    heading: "What polymyalgia rheumatica is"
    body: |
      Polymyalgia rheumatica (PMR) is one of the most common inflammatory conditions in adults over 50, with a peak incidence between 70–80 years. It is approximately twice as common in women as in men. The name describes its core features: *poly* (many) *myalgia* (muscle pain) *rheumatica* (rheumatic/inflammatory). However, the inflammation is primarily periarticular and vascular rather than truly myopathic — muscle enzymes (CK, aldolase) are normal.

      **Relationship with giant cell arteritis (GCA)**

      PMR and giant cell arteritis (temporal arteritis) share a biological relationship and frequently coexist. GCA is a large-vessel vasculitis affecting branches of the external carotid and occasionally the aorta. It is present in 15–20% of PMR patients; conversely, 40–50% of GCA patients have PMR symptoms.

      GCA is the more serious diagnosis. Inflammation in the temporal and ophthalmic arteries can cause ischaemia to the optic nerve — sudden, painless, irreversible monocular visual loss (anterior ischaemic optic neuropathy) occurs in 15–20% of untreated cases. Stroke and aortic aneurysm are rarer but serious complications.

      GCA symptoms to watch for alongside PMR:
      - New headache (typically temporal or occipital)
      - Scalp tenderness (painful to comb hair)
      - Jaw claudication (aching or fatigue in the jaw muscles on chewing)
      - Visual symptoms — blurring, diplopia, sudden visual loss
      - Tender, thickened, or nodular temporal artery

      Any patient with PMR who develops these symptoms requires urgent evaluation and high-dose steroid treatment (40–60 mg prednisolone) while awaiting temporal artery biopsy or large-vessel imaging.

      **Diagnosis**

      No single diagnostic test is specific. Diagnostic criteria require:
      - Age ≥ 50
      - Bilateral shoulder or hip girdle pain and stiffness
      - Morning stiffness lasting > 45 minutes
      - Elevated ESR and/or CRP
      - Dramatic response to prednisolone (≤ 20 mg/day) within 1–2 weeks

      Conditions to exclude: rheumatoid arthritis (hands/feet involvement, positive RF/CCP), myositis (elevated CK), polymyositis, infection, malignancy.

      **Treatment**

      *Corticosteroids:* prednisolone 15–20 mg/day is the cornerstone. Response is typically dramatic within days — if the response is poor, the diagnosis should be reconsidered. Dose is tapered slowly over 12–24 months; relapse is common if tapering is too rapid. Total treatment duration is often 1–3 years.

      *Steroid-sparing agents:* methotrexate or azathioprine may be added in relapsing or corticosteroid-dependent disease.

      *Bone protection:* long-term corticosteroids cause osteoporosis; calcium + vitamin D + bisphosphonate should be prescribed from the outset.

      *Symptom management:* physiotherapy, pain management interventions, and low-impact exercise support function during the recovery period.
