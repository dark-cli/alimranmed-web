  - type: prose
    heading: What postherpetic neuralgia is
    body: >
      Postherpetic neuralgia (PHN) is a neuropathic pain syndrome that occurs following an outbreak of varicella zoster
      virus (VZV), or "shingles." This is the same virus that causes chickenpox. During the initial infection, the virus
      remains in the body, lying dormant inside nerve cells. Years later, advanced age, illness, stress, decreased immune
      system function, or medications can reactivate the virus, causing the shingles outbreak. Sometimes, there is no
      apparent reason for the outbreak.


      Once reactivated, the virus travels along nerve fibres, causing pain. When the virus reaches the skin, it produces
      a rash and blisters. A shingles outbreak usually resolves within a month or so. But some people continue to feel
      pain long after the rash and blisters heal. This pain is known as postherpetic neuralgia.


      Not everyone who has had a reactivation of the virus develops postherpetic neuralgia. But PHN is a common
      complication of shingles in older adults. The greater your age when the virus reactivates, the greater the chance
      you will develop PHN. In most people, the pain of PHN lessens over time.
  - type: prose
    heading: Signs and symptoms
    body: >
      Neuralgia is generally limited to the area of skin where the shingles outbreak first occurred. Symptoms may include:


      - Sharp and jabbing, burning, or deep and aching pain

      - Extreme sensitivity to touch and temperature change

      - Itching and numbness

      - Headaches
  - type: prose
    heading: Causes and risk factors
    body: >
      Postherpetic neuralgia results when nerve fibres are damaged during an outbreak of shingles. Damaged fibres are
      not able to send messages from the skin to the brain as they normally do. Instead, the messages become confused and
      exaggerated, causing chronic, often excruciating pain that may persist for months — or even years — in the area
      where shingles first occurred.


      This complication of shingles occurs much more frequently in older adults. Less than 10 percent of people younger
      than 60 develop postherpetic neuralgia after a bout of shingles, while about 40 percent of people older than 60 do.
  - type: prose
    heading: When to seek medical advice
    body: >
      See a doctor at the first sign of shingles. Treating shingles early — within three days of developing the rash —
      with oral antiviral drugs may actually prevent PHN. If you do develop PHN, see your doctor right away. Finding an
      effective treatment to relieve the pain can sometimes be frustrating. You may have to work with your doctor and
      sometimes other specialists to try a variety of treatments before you find something that helps.
  - type: panels
    heading: Treatment options
    intro: >-
      Once PHN has occurred, a comprehensive, multidisciplinary pain management-oriented approach is helpful.
    panels:
      - title: Topical therapies
        items:
          - Lidocaine (Lidoderm) skin patches — prescription patches applied directly to painful skin
          - Capsaicin cream (Capzasin-P, Zostrix) — made from hot chili pepper seeds; may irritate skin initially
      - title: Medications
        items:
          - Antidepressants — affect serotonin and norepinephrine to modulate pain interpretation
          - Anticonvulsants — gabapentin (Neurontin), pregabalin (Lyrica) stabilise abnormal nerve electrical activity
          - Opiates — tramadol (Ultram) or oxycodone (OxyContin) for severe pain; may be habit-forming
      - title: Interventional procedures
        items:
          - Steroid injections — corticosteroid injected around the spinal cord area
          - Transcutaneous electrical nerve stimulation (TENS)
          - Pulsed radiofrequency ablation
    note: >-
      In some cases, treatment brings complete pain relief. But most people still experience some pain, and a few do not
      receive any relief. Although some people must live with postherpetic neuralgia for the rest of their lives, most
      people can expect the condition to gradually disappear during the first three months.
