      - label: "What it is"
        value: "A hereditary haemoglobinopathy caused by a point mutation in the β-globin gene (HbS), producing abnormal haemoglobin that polymerises when deoxygenated. The resulting rigid, sickle-shaped red blood cells have a shortened lifespan (10–20 days vs the normal 120 days), cause haemolytic anaemia, and obstruct the microcirculation — triggering ischaemia, infarction, and pain. Autosomal recessive inheritance: both alleles must be abnormal for disease expression."
      - label: "Symptoms"
        value: "Chronic haemolytic anaemia: fatigue, pallor, jaundice. Vaso-occlusive crises: acute, severe pain in bones, joints, chest, and abdomen — triggered by cold, dehydration, infection, or stress. Dactylitis (painful swelling of hands and feet) in young children. Acute chest syndrome: chest pain, fever, respiratory distress — a leading cause of death. Stroke. Splenic sequestration. Priapism. Progressive organ damage: kidneys, lungs (pulmonary hypertension), eyes (retinal ischaemia), liver. Recurrent bacterial infections from functional asplenia."
      - label: "Approach"
        value: "Preventive: hydroxyurea (increases fetal haemoglobin HbF, reduces crises by ~50%), folic acid supplementation, penicillin prophylaxis in children, pneumococcal and meningococcal vaccination. Acute crisis: hydration, analgesia (opioids for severe crises), oxygen if hypoxic, blood transfusion for acute chest syndrome and stroke. Disease-modifying: regular exchange transfusions to maintain HbS < 30% in high-risk patients. Curative: allogeneic stem cell transplant in eligible patients (usually children with severe disease and a matched sibling donor)."
  - type: prose
    heading: "What sickle cell anaemia is"
    body: |
      Normal red blood cells are biconcave discs that remain flexible as they pass through the microvasculature. They carry oxygen via haemoglobin (Hb) and live approximately 120 days. In sickle cell anaemia, a single amino acid substitution (valine for glutamic acid at position 6 of the β-globin chain) produces haemoglobin S (HbS). When HbS is deoxygenated, it polymerises into long rigid fibres, distorting the red cell into a crescent (sickle) shape.

      Sickle cells are:
      - Rigid and non-deformable — they cannot pass through small capillaries
      - Adhesive — they adhere to the vascular endothelium and to each other
      - Short-lived — haemolysed after 10–20 days, causing chronic anaemia

      **Genetics**

      Sickle cell anaemia is autosomal recessive — both copies of the HbB gene must carry the HbS mutation. Sickle cell trait (one normal copy + one HbS copy) is a carrier state; carriers rarely have symptoms but can pass the gene to their children.

      **Complications**

      *Vaso-occlusive crises:* the most common acute manifestation. Sickling in the microcirculation causes ischaemia, infarction, and pain — most often in bones (especially the femoral head), joints, chest, and abdomen. Episodes last hours to weeks and vary greatly in severity.

      *Acute chest syndrome:* new chest infiltrate on chest X-ray + symptoms (chest pain, fever, hypoxia). The most common cause of death in adults. Requires urgent transfusion and oxygen; ventilatory support in severe cases.

      *Stroke:* occurs in ~10% of children with sickle cell disease; silent cerebral infarction is more common. Chronic transfusion to maintain HbS < 30% reduces stroke risk by 90%.

      *Avascular necrosis:* ischaemic infarction of the femoral and humeral heads, causing chronic joint pain and disability.

      *Pulmonary hypertension:* a late complication resulting from chronic haemolysis and recurrent pulmonary vascular damage; affects ~10% of adults and significantly reduces survival.

      *Splenic dysfunction:* recurrent sickling in the spleen causes progressive infarction (functional asplenia by childhood), predisposing to life-threatening encapsulated bacterial infections (pneumococcus, meningococcus, Haemophilus influenzae).

      **Disease-modifying and curative treatment**

      *Hydroxyurea:* the most important disease-modifying drug. Increases fetal haemoglobin (HbF), which does not polymerise; dramatically reduces vaso-occlusive crises, acute chest syndrome, and hospitalisation. Used lifelong in moderate-to-severe disease.

      *L-glutamine, crizanlizumab, voxelotor:* newer agents that reduce adhesion, crises, and haemolysis.

      *Exchange transfusion:* replaces HbS-containing blood with normal donor blood; used acutely for stroke, acute chest syndrome, and pre-operatively; used chronically in high-risk patients.

      *Stem cell transplant:* the only established cure; limited by donor availability (best results with matched sibling), treatment-related toxicity, and patient eligibility. Gene therapy is under active investigation.

      **Pain management**

      Vaso-occlusive crises require multimodal analgesia: NSAIDs and paracetamol for mild episodes; opioids for moderate-to-severe crises; patient-controlled analgesia (PCA) during hospital admissions. Chronic pain — from avascular necrosis, neuropathy, and organ damage — is common and requires chronic pain management approaches including neuromodulation.
